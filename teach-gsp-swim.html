How to Teach a German Shorthaired Pointer to Swim: Fixing Upright Paddling, Dock and Boat Exits, and Life Jacket Fit

Upright splashing, a sinking back end and no idea how to get out are normal first-season problems. Here is the calm, step-by-step way GSP owners fix them.

Published: 2026-11-11 · German Shorthaired Pointer Club

Teaching a German Shorthaired Pointer to swim usually starts with a surprise: the dog that charges every puddle on the walk hits deep water, throws its head up and slaps the surface with its front paws like it is climbing an invisible ladder. If that is your dog, you are in good company. Plenty of GSP owners have watched the same thing happen, and the fix is a short, patient plan rather than a natural gift your dog is missing.

Quick answer: Start in warm, calm, shallow water where you can stand. Put your GSP in a snug life jacket with a back handle, support its belly or hold the handle so the body levels out, and let it swim a few strokes toward a clear exit. Lengthen the distance only when the stroke is quiet and flat. Teach every exit you will use (pool steps, a dock ladder or ramp, a boat platform) on purpose, and keep sessions short so the dog ends wanting more.

The stages below follow the order that experienced pointing-dog trainers and water-safety guides keep coming back to: comfort in the shallows first, buoyancy and support second, short swims to a known exit third, and longer swims last. You do not need a lake house or a perfect retriever to get there, only a few calm sessions a week.

If you want to see the early steps before you try them, this trainer video from American Standard Dog Training walks through building comfort near the water, the first supported swims in a pool, what to do if a dog falls in, and using a leash to guide the dog back to the exit.

Watch how the trainer keeps pointing the dog back toward the steps. That habit matters more than any single swim, and it is the part most of us skip when an excited bird dog is already in the water.

Why does a GSP paddle upright instead of swimming flat?

Most GSPs paddle upright because they are trying to keep their head as high as possible and only use their front legs, so the rear end sinks and the body goes vertical. It is a beginner pattern, not a fault in the breed, and it usually fades once the dog relaxes and starts driving with the back legs.

The Whole Dog Journal's swimming guide puts it bluntly: dogs do not automatically know how to swim, and the classic "dog paddle" is more of a survival reaction than a real stroke, and a very inefficient way to move through water. You will often see the same picture in a young GSP: a dog that charges happily through the shallows, then, once it cannot touch the bottom, lifts its head and splashes hard with its front paws without getting very far.

What the splash is telling you

Upright paddling burns energy fast. The dog works hard, moves slowly and often panics a little, which makes it lift the head even higher. A lean, deep-chested, short-coated dog like a GSP has little natural padding to keep the back end up, so the "climbing" posture shows up a lot in young pointers. The good news: buoyancy from a jacket and a little support under the middle let the back end come up, so the rear legs can start doing their share.

How do you fix upright paddling in a German Shorthaired Pointer?

Give the dog buoyancy and support so the body levels out, then let it swim very short distances toward an exit it already knows. Repetition at that easy level teaches the back legs to join in; force and long swims do the opposite.

Step 1: Make the shallows boring

Start where your dog can wade and you can stand comfortably. Gary Satterfield, who has trained pointing breeds professionally for 33 years, told Gun Dog magazine that he waits until pups are at least four months old, walks them along a shallow stream, wades in himself and praises them for following. He avoids force of any kind and only works in warm weather and warm water, because a cold-water shock can leave a young dog wary of water for life.

Step 2: Support the middle, not the head

Once your GSP is calm in water up to its chest, carry or walk it a step deeper wearing its jacket. Hold the back handle or slide a flat hand under the belly, point the dog toward the exit and let go once it is relaxed. That is the same approach the Whole Dog Journal guide describes. Do not hold the head up: lifting the front end makes the vertical posture worse.

Step 3: Lengthen the swim gradually

Satterfield's progression is a useful model for any pointer family: first wading across the stream, then crossing at a spot where the pup has to swim a few strokes in the middle, then gradually longer swims as the pup grows keen. Add distance only when the stroke stays quiet for the whole swim. If the splashing comes back, you went too far; shorten the next swim.

Step 4: Use a toy for direction, not distance

A floating toy tossed one or two body lengths toward the exit gives a bird dog something to aim at and keeps the head forward instead of up. Long throws into open water tempt a tired beginner to keep going past what its stroke can handle.

How should a life jacket fit a GSP?

A GSP life jacket should be snug but comfortable, stay in place when you lift the dog by the handle, and leave the front legs free to reach. If it rides toward the ears, rubs behind the elbows or sits on the groin, it is the wrong size or the wrong cut.

The American Animal Hospital Association's water safety tips for pets recommend a jacket that fits snugly but comfortably and lets the pet move freely without slipping or restricting it, ideally brightly colored or reflective with a handle on the back so you can spot your dog and pull it out of the water if needed.

Measure first, then read the brand's chart

Measure the chest girth with a soft tape around the widest part of the ribcage, just behind the front legs, with your dog standing. GSPs are deep-chested and narrow-waisted, so a jacket can fit the chest and still slide back on the waist. Size charts differ a lot between brands, which is why the planner above asks for the girth range on your jacket's own chart rather than a generic size.

Five fit checks before the first swim

  • Handle lift: lift your dog a few inches by the handle on dry land. The jacket should stay put and the dog should hang level, not slide out of the front.
  • Reach: watch your dog trot. The front legs need a full stride without the chest panel catching them.
  • Belly strap: the rear strap should sit in front of the groin, not across it. Check this on males in particular, and make sure no strap pinches when the dog kicks.
  • Snug, not tight: tighten every strap, then try to pull the jacket forward over the shoulders. It should not come off.
  • Visibility: a bright color helps when a liver-and-white head disappears into a dark lake.

Introduce the jacket on dry land

Put the jacket on at home for a meal, a short play session and a calm settle on the mat before it ever gets wet. A GSP that already links the jacket with good things is far less likely to freeze or bolt the first time it floats.

How do you teach a GSP to get out of the pool, off a dock or onto a boat?

Teach each exit as its own skill in calm conditions: walk the dog in and out of it on leash, reward the dog for finding it, then start short swims that always end at that exit. Never assume a dog that swims well can climb out of deep water on its own.

AAHA notes that a pet ramp or pool steps, plus teaching your pet to use them, helps a tired or disoriented dog get out safely and reduces the risk of panic or drowning. The Whole Dog Journal guide adds a simple trick for pools: build a positive link with the steps by feeding treats or offering a toy on the top step, so your dog knows where the way out is.

Pool steps

Walk your GSP in and out of the steps on leash several times before any swim. Then start swims a couple of feet from the steps, always facing them, and widen the circle slowly. Dogs that only know the wall try to claw out over the edge, which is exhausting and dangerous.

Docks

Most docks are too high for a dog to climb. Use a floating dog ramp or a ladder made for dogs, practice the ramp in shallow water first, and expect to lift the back end by the jacket handle the first few times. Keep the dog on a long line near a dock until its recall is reliable around distractions; ducks and geese turn a pointer's ears off fast.

Boats

Treat boat exits as a two-person job. One person keeps the dog from swimming toward the stern and anything moving, the other takes the jacket handle and guides front paws onto the platform or ramp. A GSP that has learned "paws up, then lift" at the dock usually transfers it to the boat after a few calm repeats.

Step-by-step: a first-season swim plan for your GSP

Use this checklist across a few weeks. Stay on a step until the dog does it calmly two sessions in a row, and only change one thing at a time.

  1. Dry-land jacket practice: wear the jacket indoors and on a walk until your dog ignores it.
  2. Shallow wading: warm, calm water, you in the water first, praise for following. No pulling or throwing in.
  3. Exit rehearsal: walk in and out of the exit you will use, on leash, with treats at the top.
  4. Supported float: one step out of depth, hand under the belly or on the handle, nose pointed at the exit, release when relaxed.
  5. Short swims to the exit: two or three body lengths, end on a calm swim and a reward at the exit.
  6. Longer swims: add a little distance per session, only while the stroke stays level and quiet.
  7. Exit drills from anywhere: start swims from different angles so your dog always finds the way out.
  8. Fun work: short retrieves near shore, then a rinse, a drink of fresh water and a rest.

If you already juggle walks, training and nose games, slot two or three short swims into the GSP weekly planner instead of one long weekend marathon. Young dogs in particular do better with short, frequent sessions; the puppy exercise limits guide explains why growing pointers need brakes on total activity, and swimming counts toward it.

Which mistakes slow down a GSP's swimming?

The most common mistakes are rushing into deep water, skipping the jacket because "pointers are natural swimmers," and never teaching the exit. Each one teaches the dog that water is hard work or scary.

  • Throwing or carrying the dog into deep water. The Whole Dog Journal guide warns never to throw a dog in thinking it will figure it out. Satterfield also lists force as a classic novice mistake with pointing breeds.
  • Starting too young or in cold water. Satterfield waits until at least four months and works in warm water only.
  • Lifting the head to "help." Support goes under the belly or on the handle; holding the chin up makes the dog more vertical.
  • Long swims while the stroke is still upright. Tired dogs splash more, and splashing tires them faster.
  • Only practicing the fun part. Retrieves are exciting, but the dog also needs to know the way out of every place it swims.
  • Swimming in water you have not checked. See the safety checks below.

What should you check before every swim?

Check the water, the exit and your dog's energy before every session: clear water without scum, an exit you have rehearsed, and a dog that is still keen rather than flat or shivering.

Blue-green algae is the big hidden risk in lakes and ponds. The ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center explains that it thrives in fresh water when the weather is warm (over 75 degrees) and sunny, often looks like pea-green paint or slime on the surface, and can poison dogs that drink from or simply swim in it. Algae cells can also stick to fur and be swallowed when the dog licks itself, so rinse your GSP with fresh water after a swim, and watch for posted warning signs.

A quick pre-swim checklist

  • Water clear, no green scum along the shore, no warning signs.
  • No strong current, boat traffic or steep drop-offs at the entry point.
  • Jacket on, straps checked, handle within reach.
  • Fresh drinking water packed so the dog is not tempted to drink the lake.
  • Plan to stop while your dog still wants more, then settle on a towel.

Frequently asked questions about teaching a GSP to swim

Are German Shorthaired Pointers natural swimmers?

Many GSPs love water and become strong swimmers, but they still have to learn an efficient stroke. Upright, splashy paddling is common in beginners and usually improves with a life jacket, support and short swims.

At what age can a GSP puppy start swimming?

Pointing-breed trainer Gary Satterfield, quoted in Gun Dog magazine, waits until pups are at least four months old, when balance and coordination are better, and starts with wading in warm, shallow water.

Does my GSP need a life jacket if it already swims?

A jacket helps any dog that is still learning, and it gives you a handle for dock and boat exits. Many owners keep using one around boats, deep water and docks even after the stroke is level.

Why does my GSP's back end sink when it swims?

The dog is paddling mostly with its front legs and holding its head high. Support under the belly or a buoyant jacket lets the body level out so the back legs can start pushing.

How do I get my GSP to use the pool steps instead of the wall?

Walk it in and out of the steps on leash, reward it on the top step, and start every swim close to the steps and facing them. Widen the starting distance slowly from different sides of the pool.

How do I get a wet GSP back onto a boat?

Turn the engine off, guide the dog to the swim platform or boarding ramp, let it put its front paws up and lift by the jacket handle. Practice the same "paws up, then lift" at a dock first.

Every pointer gets there at its own pace

Teaching a German Shorthaired Pointer to swim is less about the dog's instincts and more about a few calm habits: warm, shallow starts, a jacket that fits and stays put, support under the belly instead of the chin, short swims that always end at a known exit, and a quick look at the water before you go. Do that a few times a week and the upright splash usually turns into the quiet, level stroke GSP owners love to watch. Use the planner after each session, stay at a stage until the stroke is calm, and enjoy the season with your dog.
